# Idea fact-check and YouTube crowding check (8 Nov 2026)

Seven "What if…" ideas. Each one has verified facts with sources, disputed points, popular claims to avoid, a payoff number, and a YouTube crowding check.

## Summary

| # | Idea | Verdict | Best payoff number | Biggest accuracy trap |
|---|---|---|---|---|

## 1. What if your twin travelled at light speed?

### Verified facts

| # | Fact | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Lorentz factor γ = 1/√(1−v²/c²). **At 99.5% c, γ = 10.01**: each year on the ship is about 10 years on Earth. At 99.9% c, γ = 22.4. At 99.94% c, γ = 28.9. (Our arithmetic from the standard formula.) | [HyperPhysics, Georgia State Univ., time dilation](http://hyperphysics.phy-astr.gsu.edu/hbase/Relativ/tdil.html) |
| 2 | **Muons in CERN's storage ring at γ = 29.33 (about 99.94% c) lived 29.3× longer than muons at rest.** The measured ratio matched the relativity prediction to within (2 ± 9)×10⁻⁴. | Bailey et al., *Nature* 268, 301–305 (1977), [nature.com/articles/268301a0](https://www.nature.com/articles/268301a0) |
| 3 | **GPS clocks run fast by about 38.6 µs per day.** Weaker gravity adds about +45.7 µs/day (fractional shift 5.288×10⁻¹⁰). Orbital speed subtracts about −7.1 µs/day (−8.228×10⁻¹¹). The clocks are tuned low by the net 4.4647×10⁻¹⁰ before launch. Ashby: left uncorrected, the gravitational part alone would build up to a **13.7 km** navigation error in a day. | Neil Ashby (Univ. Colorado), [*Relativistic Effects in the GPS*](https://www.aapt.org/doorway/tgru/articles/ashbyarticle.pdf). Per-day µs values are our conversion of his fractional shifts |
| 4 | Hafele–Keating, 1971: atomic clocks flown around the world on airliners. Predicted: −40 ± 23 ns eastward and +275 ± 21 ns westward. Measured: **−59 ± 10 ns east, +273 ± 7 ns west.** | [HyperPhysics: Hafele–Keating](https://hyperphysics.gsu.edu/hbase/Relativ/airtim.html). Original: *Science* 177 (1972) |
| 5 | Scott Kelly spent **340 consecutive days** on the ISS (launched March 2015). His identical twin Mark stayed on the ground as the control. Ten research teams took part. | [NASA biography PDF](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/kellysj.pdf); [NASA Twins Study](https://www.nasa.gov/humans-in-space/twins-study/) |
| 6 | **The age gap.** Mark Kelly said in July 2016 that he is now "6 minutes and 5 milliseconds" older (he was born 6 minutes first). A standard calculation for the 340 days gives about **8.4–8.6 ms**: ISS speed effect about −3.26×10⁻¹⁰, gravity about +0.41×10⁻¹⁰. The two sources disagree, so say **"a few thousandths of a second"**. | [Space.com, July 2016](https://space.com/33411-astronaut-scott-kelly-relativity-twin-brother-ages.html); [Quartz](https://qz.com/370729/astronaut-scott-kelly-will-return-from-a-year-in-space-both-older-and-younger-than-his-twin-brother) (8.6 ms) |
| 7 | Cosmonaut Sergei Krikalev logged **803 days** in orbit, which works out to about **0.02 s (1/50 s)** of time dilation. | [FAI record note](https://fai.org/node/24726) |
| 8 | **Twins Study biology.** Published as Garrett-Bakelman et al., *Science* 2019. Scott's telomeres lengthened in flight, then shortened after landing. In NASA's March 2018 clarification, **93%** of the gene-expression changes had returned to normal after landing. The other **7%** of *changed expression* (not DNA) still persisted six months later. | [PubMed 30975860](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30975860/); [Space.com on NASA's clarification](https://www.space.com/40007-astronauts-scott-mark-kelly-still-identical.html) |

### Uncertain or disputed

- **The exact Kelly gap (5 ms vs 8.6 ms).** It was never measured; it is calculated. Use "a few thousandths of a second" or "less than a hundredth of a second".
- **Ashby's 13.7 km/day** is for the gravitational shift alone. Combined with the velocity part it is about 11.6 km/day, which is our subtraction. A real uncorrected system would degrade in more complicated ways. Say "an error of kilometres per day".

### Popular claims that are wrong

- "At light speed, time stops for you." Nothing with mass reaches c. Use 99.5% c.
- "The twin paradox is a contradiction" or "it needs general relativity." The asymmetry comes from the travelling twin changing inertial frames when they turn around. Special relativity is enough ([Usenet Physics FAQ, UC Riverside](https://math.ucr.edu/home/baez/physics/Relativity/SR/TwinParadox/twin_paradox.html)).
- "7% of Scott Kelly's DNA changed" or "they're no longer identical twins." It was gene expression, and NASA corrected the reports.
- "Space made Scott biologically younger" (because of telomeres). The telomeres returned to normal after landing. The relativity effect is milliseconds.
- "On the ISS, gravity is why astronauts age slower." On the ISS, speed dominates, so they age slower. On GPS satellites, gravity dominates, so the clocks run faster. Don't mix the two up.

### Payoff number

**29.3×.** Muons racing at 99.94% of light speed lived 29 times longer than muons at rest ([Bailey et al. 1977](https://www.nature.com/articles/268301a0)). For the human beat, use Scott Kelly coming home "a few milliseconds" younger than his twin.

## 2. What if you were born on Mars?

### Verified facts

| # | Fact | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Mars surface gravity is 3.73 m/s², 0.380× Earth's.** A day is 24.66 h and a year is 687 Earth days. | [NASA NSSDC Mars fact sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/marsfact.html) |
| 2 | In microgravity, bones lose **1–1.5% of their density per month** on 4–6-month missions. | [NASA HRP: Risk of spaceflight-induced bone changes](https://www.nasa.gov/reference/risk-of-spaceflight-induced-bone-changes/) |
| 3 | **17 astronauts were tracked for a year after landing. 9 of the 17 had not recovered tibia bone density.** Strength, density and microarchitecture were still 0.9–2.1% below pre-flight. The paper calls this commensurate with "a decade or more" of age-related bone loss. | Gabel et al., *Sci. Rep.* 12:9446 (2022), via [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2022/06/220630134845.htm) |
| 4 | Crew members grow **up to 3% taller** on long missions (about 5 cm for a 6-foot person) as the spine unloads. They return to normal height on Earth. | [NASA ISS Spinal Ultrasound investigation](https://www.nasa.gov/?p=420143); [Space.com](https://www.space.com/19116-astronauts-taller-space-spines.html) |
| 5 | Astronauts can lose **up to 20% of muscle mass on flights of 5–11 days**. | [Canadian Space Agency](https://asc-csa.gc.ca/eng/sciences/osm/muscles.asp) |
| 6 | **Heart.** The heart became **9.4% more spherical** in space (12 astronauts, 2014). Scott Kelly's left ventricle lost about **0.74 g of mass per week** over his 340 days, despite exercising six days a week. Its diastolic diameter fell from 5.3 to 4.6 cm. | [ACC press release 2014](https://www.acc.org/about-acc/press-releases/2014/03/29/09/09/may-hearts-in-space); MacNamara/Levine, *Circulation* 2021, via [UT Southwestern](https://www.utsouthwestern.edu/newsroom/articles/year-2021/astronauts-heart-left-smaller.html) |
| 7 | **No mammal has ever been gestated in space or partial gravity from start to finish.** NASA's NIH.R1/R2 flights launched pregnant rats at gestation day 9 or 11 and landed them at day 20 of 22; they gave birth on Earth. The pups showed vestibular (inner-ear balance) differences. Mouse embryos cultured on the ISS reached only the blastocyst stage, after 4 days (Wakayama, *iScience* 2023). | [NatGeo 2018 (April Ronca)](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/can-humans-have-babies-on-mars-space-it-may-be-harder-than-you-think); [NTRS mission report](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19990053345/downloads/19990053345.pdf); [AFP via Gulf News](https://gulfnews.com/world/asia/mouse-embryos-grown-in-space-for-first-time-japan-researchers-1.99056825) |
| 8 | **The only partial-gravity animal data.** Mice on the ISS centrifuge at lunar gravity (1/6 g) were protected from soleus muscle atrophy, but not from the shift in muscle fibre type. The authors suggest a threshold somewhere between 1/6 g and 1 g. | JAXA, *Communications Biology* (Apr 2023), [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/616b4f5eef8e49edb9316c884498acae) |

### Known vs speculation (say this clearly in the video)

| Known | Unknown or speculation |
|---|---|
| What 0 g does to adults over months: the bone, muscle, heart and spine numbers above | What **0.38 g** does to anyone, over any length of time |
| Astronauts readapt to 1 g after 340 days in 0 g | Whether a child's skeleton and heart would grow strong enough for Earth if they grew up in 0.38 g |
| Rat fetuses carried in orbit from mid-pregnancy were born on Earth | Whether human pregnancy and birth work at 0.38 g, and what Mars radiation does to a fetus |

Bottom line: "**Could a Mars-born person survive Earth?** Nobody knows. It has never been tested, not even in a mouse."

### Popular claims that are wrong

- Using astronaut bone-loss rates as Mars rates. They are 0 g rates.
- "Mars-born humans would be 3 m tall / giants." Speculation.
- "Earth would kill them instantly." Speculation. The best analogue, astronauts after a year in 0 g, survive.
- "Babies have been born in space." None have.
- "Astronauts permanently grow 2 inches." The height gain is temporary.

### Payoff number

**About a decade.** A year after coming home, astronauts were still missing bone equal to roughly a decade of ageing on Earth ([Gabel et al. 2022](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2022/06/220630134845.htm)).

## 3. What if you remembered every day of your life? (HSAM)

### Verified facts

| # | Fact | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The first case was "AJ", later named publicly as **Jill Price** (born 1965). The paper was Parker, Cahill & McGaugh, "A case of unusual autobiographical remembering", *Neurocase* 12:35–49 (2006). | [Paper PDF (Gwern mirror)](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2006-parker.pdf) |
| 2 | Her recall became "automatic" **from 1980, at age 14**. From 1974 to 1979 (ages 8 to 13) she remembers many days, but not every day. She kept diaries from age 10 to age 34, which the researchers used to check her answers. | Parker et al. 2006 (above) |
| 3 | Her email to McGaugh: "**non-stop, uncontrollable and totally exhausting.**" She described it as "a running movie that never stops… like a split screen." | Parker et al. 2006 |
| 4 | **The Easter test (2003).** With no warning, she wrote down all **24 Easter dates from 1980 to 2003 in 10 minutes. All but one were right, and that one was off by two days.** She also noted what she did each weekend. She is Jewish. | Parker et al. 2006 |
| 5 | She "had great difficulty with **rote memorization**" at school. This is not photographic memory. | Parker et al. 2006 |
| 6 | **How many cases.** By July 2012, UC Irvine had assessed **500+** people and confirmed **33**. That study of 11 found differences in 9 brain structures and some obsessive-compulsive tendencies. Later estimates of about **60 worldwide** come from secondary sources (see the disputed list). | [UCI News, 30 Jul 2012](https://news.uci.edu/?p=4482) (LePort et al., *Neurobiol. Learn. Mem.* 2012) |
| 7 | People with HSAM **still form false memories.** In a misinformation task (20 HSAM vs 38 controls), they showed *more* overall false memory than controls. | Patihis et al., *PNAS* 2013, [PMC3876270 commentary](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3876270) / [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/11/131119131438.htm) |
| 8 | **Forgetting is an active process.** In fruit flies, lowering the activity of the protein Rac stretched an early memory **from a few hours to more than a day**; raising it sped forgetting (Shuai et al., *Cell* 2010). Activating a small set of dopamine neurons after learning **erased** a memory and inhibiting them **enhanced** it; mutating the DAMB receptor blocked forgetting (Berry et al., *Neuron* 2012). In adult mice, boosting new hippocampal neurons after learning caused forgetting (Akers et al., *Science* 2014). | [CSHL release](https://www.cshl.edu/cshl-neuroscientist-discovers-protein-that-regulates-forgetting-of-short-term-memories/); [PubMed 22578504](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22578504/); [Nature News](https://www.nature.com/news/new-brain-cells-erase-old-memories-1.15186) |

### Uncertain or disputed

- **The worldwide count.** "61 as of 2016" (Wikipedia) and "62 as of 2024" (a 2025 review) are secondary figures, and there is no official registry. Say "only a few dozen confirmed".
- **Whether diary-keeping or obsessive rehearsal explains HSAM** is debated. Price and McGaugh reject that explanation.
- **The forgetting mechanisms** come from flies and mice. They are not shown in people.

### Popular claims that are wrong

- "Photographic or eidetic memory." HSAM is autobiographical, organised by date.
- "They remember every second" or "every detail perfectly." The memory is selective, and they form false memories.
- "Hyperthymesia is a recognised disorder (DSM)." It isn't in the DSM.
- "Jill Price is the only one."

### Payoff number

**24 Easters in 10 minutes, with one miss, off by two days** ([Parker et al. 2006](https://www.gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2006-parker.pdf)). Easter moves every year.

## 4. What if you woke up every morning with no memory?

### Verified facts

| # | Fact | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Clive Wearing**, an English musician and musicologist in his mid-forties, caught **herpes encephalitis in March 1985**. It left him with "a memory span of only seconds", which Oliver Sacks called "the most devastating case of amnesia ever recorded". He also lost almost all his past (retrograde amnesia): he told Deborah he had never heard of John Lennon or JFK. | Oliver Sacks, "The Abyss", *The New Yorker*, 24 Sep 2007 ([PDF copy, Univ. at Buffalo](https://cse.buffalo.edu/~rapaport/575/F08/sacks07-nykr.pdf)) |
| 2 | **Diary (verbatim, via Sacks):** "2:10 P.M: This time properly awake. . . . 2:14 P.M: this time finally awake. . . . 2:35 P.M: this time completely awake". Each entry was crossed out by the next. The journal grew to "hundreds of almost identical pages". | Sacks 2007 |
| 3 | In 1990 he said: "Can you imagine one night five years long? … It's like being dead." He left Deborah phone messages: "Please come and see me, darling—it's been ages… Please fly here at the speed of light." | Sacks 2007 |
| 4 | He **always recognised Deborah** as his wife and greeted her with passionate relief every time. He can still sight-read, play the piano and organ, sing and conduct. | Sacks 2007; Wilson, Baddeley & Kapur, *J. Clin. Exp. Neuropsychol.* 17:668–681 (1995), [MRC CBU record](https://www.mrc-cbu.cam.ac.uk/bibliography/articles/3367) |
| 5 | **Henry Molaison ("H.M.")** had bilateral medial temporal lobe surgery for epilepsy at **age 27, on 1 Sept 1953**, by William Scoville at Hartford Hospital. It was reported by Scoville & Milner in 1957. He **died on 2 Dec 2008, aged 82.** Suzanne Corkin studied him from 1962, for about 46 years. | [NYRB](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/2017/08/17/making-memories-patient-hm/); [The Tech obituary of Corkin](https://thetech.com/2016/06/02/suzanne-corkin-scientist-obituary); [McGill](https://www.mcgill.ca/newsroom/node/17008) |
| 6 | He **learned mirror drawing over 3 days with no memory of having practised.** This was the classic split between knowing *how* and knowing *that* (Milner 1962). | [APS, *Psychological Science*](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/?p=99819) |
| 7 | **How much was removed.** Scoville estimated 8 cm. MRI showed about **5.4 cm (left) and 5.1 cm (right)**, with about **2 cm of posterior hippocampus spared**. After his death his brain was cut into **2,401 sections, each 70 µm thick** (Annese et al., *Nat. Commun.* 2014). | Corkin et al., *J. Neurosci.* 1997, [MGH record](https://nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/node/3818); [Live Science](https://www.livescience.com/42898-patient-hm-postmortem-brain.html) |
| 8 | **The only documented "overnight reset" case is patient FL** (after a car crash in 2005). The authors judged it a *functional* amnesia, not brain damage, and the *Skeptical Inquirer* criticised the evidence. | Smith et al., *Neuropsychologia* 48:2833 (2010), [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/woman-whose-new-memories-are-erased-each-night); [CFI critique](https://cdn.centerforinquiry.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/2011/05/22164337/p35.pdf) |

### Uncertain or disputed

- **Clive's span** is described as 7, 20 or 30 seconds in different places. "7 seconds" is a documentary title. Say "**seconds**".
- **Clive's current status is unverified.** The latest coverage found was August 2024. Do not say he is alive or dead.
- **Michelle Philpots** (memory "stuck in 1994", widely reported in 2010) is often called the inspiration for *50 First Dates*. The film came out in **2004**, before her story was widely reported. Treat the link as unverified and don't use it.

### Popular claims that are wrong

- "Amnesia resets when you sleep" (*50 First Dates*). Organic amnesia loses new information in seconds to minutes, not at bedtime.
- "Clive forgot his wife." He always recognised her.
- "H.M. had his hippocampus removed completely." About 2 cm remained.
- "H.M. could learn nothing new." He learned motor skills.

### Payoff number

**Three "first" awakenings in 25 minutes**: 2:10, 2:14 and 2:35 PM ([Sacks 2007](https://cse.buffalo.edu/~rapaport/575/F08/sacks07-nykr.pdf)). The second-best number is H.M.'s brain in 2,401 slices.

## 5. What if you couldn't feel pain?

### Verified facts

| # | Fact | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Channelopathy CIP is caused by loss of Nav1.7, a sodium channel encoded by SCN9A.** Inheritance is autosomal recessive. People with it **can still tell sharp from dull and hot from cold**. Many have **no sense of smell** (anosmia). | [MedlinePlus Genetics](https://medlineplus.gov/genetics/condition/congenital-insensitivity-to-pain/) |
| 2 | **Prevalence: "rare … its exact prevalence is unknown."** | MedlinePlus (above) |
| 3 | **Cox et al., *Nature* 444:894 (2006)** mapped the condition in **3 consanguineous families in northern Pakistan** and found 3 nonsense mutations. The index case was a 10-year-old who performed "street theatre": he **put knives through his arms and walked on burning coals**. | [PubMed 17167479](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17167479/); [JAMA report](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/205186) |
| 4 | **Typical harms.** Undetected wounds, bruises and broken bones; joint deformities; vision loss from unnoticed eye injuries; possibly shorter life expectancy. In CIPA (the NTRK1 form, which also stops sweating), people bite their tongue, lips and fingers, sometimes to the point of losing tissue. They also develop Charcot joints and recurrent dangerously high fevers. | MedlinePlus [CIP](https://medlineplus.gov/genetics/condition/congenital-insensitivity-to-pain/), [CIPA](https://medlineplus.gov/genetics/condition/congenital-insensitivity-to-pain-with-anhidrosis/) |
| 5 | **Jo Cameron** (Habib et al., *Br. J. Anaesth.* 2019). At **65** she had a hip replacement and needed only paracetamol. At **66** she had a hand operation (trapeziectomy) with no painkillers beyond paracetamol, and was **diagnosed at 66**. She had a lifetime of painless cuts and burns, "often **smelling her burning flesh** before noticing any injury", and the wounds healed quickly. | [PMC6676009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6676009) |
| 6 | Her cause is a **microdeletion in the pseudogene FAAH-OUT plus a common low-activity FAAH variant**, which raises anandamide. She scored **0 out of 21** on the GAD-7 anxiety scale and reports memory lapses. Her son, who carries the microdeletion only, has milder pain insensitivity. | Habib 2019 (above); [NYT via SBS](https://www.sbs.com.au/news/article/at-71-jo-has-never-felt-pain-or-anxiety-now-scientists-know-why/mhsro6hto) |
| 7 | A 2023 follow-up found that the FAAH-OUT deletion turns FAAH down through DNA methylation. It also changed many other genes (797 up, 348 down), including **WNT16** (wound healing), **BDNF** (mood) and **ACKR3** (opioid regulation). | Mikaeili et al., *Brain* 2023, [Newswise](https://www.newswise.com/articles/study-reveals-unique-molecular-machinery-of-woman-who-can-t-feel-pain) |

### Uncertain or disputed

- **The Cox index boy's death.** He reportedly died just before his 14th birthday after jumping off a house roof. That appears in secondary write-ups of the paper, which is paywalled; I could not confirm it in the full text. Verify before you use it.
- **The CIPA frequency** "1 in 125 million" (Wikipedia) is poorly sourced. Japanese estimates differ. Use "nobody knows exactly how many".

### Popular claims that are wrong

- "They can't feel anything." Touch and temperature are intact in SCN9A CIP.
- "Jo Cameron has CIP" or "feels zero pain." She has a different mechanism, FAAH-OUT, and feels very little pain.
- "It's a superpower." Life expectancy may be shorter.
- Any specific prevalence like "1 in a million". It's unknown.

### Payoff number

**66.** Jo Cameron reached 66 before doctors realised she barely felt pain; she often smelled her burns before she noticed them ([Habib 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6676009)).

## 6. What if you stopped aging at 25?

### Verified facts

| # | Fact | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Greenland shark.** Eye-lens radiocarbon dating of **28 females (81–502 cm)** showed a lifespan of **at least 272 years**. The largest shark, at 502 cm, was **392 ± 120 years** old, a 95.4% probability range of **272–512**. Sexual maturity comes at **≥156 ± 22 years**, inferred from earlier data that females mature above 4 m. | Nielsen et al., *Science* 353:702 (2016), [Nature News](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature.2016.20406); [sci.news](https://www.sci.news/biology/greenland-sharks-longest-lived-vertebrates-04099.html) |
| 2 | **Naked mole-rat.** Over **3,000** lifespan records (3,848 animals catalogued; 447 deaths observed), the **risk of death did not rise with age**, even at 25× the age of sexual maturity. The daily hazard stayed at about **8×10⁻⁵, roughly 1 in 12,500 per day**. One animal was still alive at more than 30 years. Human mortality doubles about every **7–8.5 years**. | Ruby, Smith & Buffenstein, *eLife* 2018, [elifesciences.org/articles/31157](https://elifesciences.org/articles/31157) |
| 3 | **Hydra.** **2,256** animals over **3.9 million observation days** showed constant, very low mortality of about **0.6% per year**. At that rate, "even after 500 years five percent of a cohort will still be alive"; in 2 of the 12 cohorts it would take 3,000 years. Fertility didn't decline with age. These were lab conditions: their own dish, 18 °C, no predators. | Schaible et al., *PNAS* 2015, [MPIDR press release](https://www.demogr.mpg.de/en/news_press/news/press/forever_young_4396.htm); Martínez, *Exp. Gerontol.* 1998 ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9615920)) |
| 4 | **"Negligible senescence"** (Caleb Finch): no measurable rise in death rate, no decline in reproduction and no functional decline with age. Candidates include some rockfish, sturgeon, turtles and bivalves. | [Overview with Finch citation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negligible_senescence); [Discover](https://www.discovermagazine.com/the-sciences/negligible-senescence-why-do-some-animals-age-differently) |
| 5 | **US risk of death at age 25 (2022 life table): 0.123% per year, or 1 in 813.** Men: 0.175%. Women: 0.069%. | CDC NVSR 74(2), [Table 1 (xlsx)](https://ftp.cdc.gov/pub/Health_Statistics/NCHS/Publications/NVSR/74-02/Table01.xlsx) |
| 6 | **If that risk never rose** (our calculation): **half of a never-aging population would be dead by about age 588**, and the mean lifespan would be about 838. For men alone the median is about 422; for women alone, about 1,029. | Derived from row 5 |
| 7 | At ages 25–34, the **leading cause of death is unintentional injury: 36,972 of the deaths in 2022, mostly poisonings and overdoses (26,767)**. "Stopped aging" doesn't stop that. | [CDC WISQARS 2022](https://wisqars.cdc.gov/pdfs/leading-causes-of-death-by-age-group_2022_508.pdf) |

### Uncertain or disputed

- **The naked mole-rat result is challenged.** Dammann et al. (*eLife* 2019) argued the data are too thin past 12 years; the authors reanalysed and stood by it. Naked mole-rats **still age epigenetically**, more slowly than mice and faster than humans (Kerepesi et al., *Nat. Commun.* 2022). Cancer has been reported in them, in a 22-year-old and a roughly 20-year-old (Delaney et al., *Vet. Pathol.* 2016). Sources: [comment response](https://elifesciences.org/articles/47047), [PMC8763950](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8763950/), [Live Science](https://www.livescience.com/53753-naked-mole-rats-get-cancer.html).
- **Not all Hydra are "immortal".** A cold-sensitive strain of *Hydra oligactis* ages after sexual reproduction (Yoshida 2006; [Sun et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7394996)).
- **The shark's radiocarbon age range is wide.** Use "at least 272" or "about 400".

### Popular claims that are wrong

- "A 512-year-old shark." 512 is the top of the error range.
- "Naked mole-rats don't age / never get cancer / are immortal."
- "Hydra can't die."
- "Stop aging and you live forever."
- "Stop aging at 25 and you'd live 700 years." That number appears in a competing Short. It's plausible for a constant risk of about 0.1%, but it depends on the year and sex used. Our 2022 CDC figure gives a median of 588.

### Payoff number

**588.** Freeze your body at 25 and you'd still face a 1-in-813 chance of dying each year, mostly from accidents. Half of you would be gone by about 588 ([CDC 2022 life table](https://ftp.cdc.gov/pub/Health_Statistics/NCHS/Publications/NVSR/74-02/Table01.xlsx); our arithmetic). Alternative: a Greenland shark isn't sexually mature until **about 150** ([Nielsen 2016](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature.2016.20406)).

## 7. What if you felt everyone else's pain? (mirror-touch)

### Verified facts

| # | Fact | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Prevalence.** Banissy et al. (*Exp. Brain Res.* 198:261, 2009) screened **567 UK undergraduates**. **10.8%** self-reported mirror-touch, **2.5%** were still in after interviews, and **1.6%** (about 1 in 60) were confirmed by a visual–tactile congruency test. | [Chun & Hupé 2013 review (Frontiers)](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00776/pdf); [Bristol record](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/publications/prevalence-characteristics-and-a-neurocognitive-model-of-mirror-t/) |
| 2 | **What it is.** Seeing someone touched produces touch on the **same part of your own body**. fMRI of the first case, "C", showed **higher somatosensory cortex activation** when watching touch than in controls; the authors read her touch-mirror system as overactive. | Blakemore et al., *Brain* 128:1571 (2005), [PubMed 15817510](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15817510/) |
| 3 | **Empathy link.** In 10 mirror-touch synaesthetes vs controls, scores were higher only on the **emotional reactivity** subscale of the Empathy Quotient, not on cognitive empathy or social skills. | Banissy & Ward, *Nat. Neurosci.* 10:815 (2007), [Goldsmiths record](https://research.gold.ac.uk/id/eprint/5991) |
| 4 | **The empathy link "has been disputed".** A 2018 study using a new screening tool found greater *emotional* empathy and better recognition of facial expressions, but typical scores on other empathy measures. | Ward, Schnakenberg & Banissy, *Cogn. Neuropsychol.* 2018, [PubMed 29770733](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29770733/) |
| 5 | **How it works: two theories.** (a) A threshold theory: overactivity in the brain's touch-mirroring system. (b) A self–other theory: a weaker boundary between self and other, involving the temporoparietal junction and medial prefrontal cortex. They are not mutually exclusive. | Ward & Banissy, *Cogn. Neurosci.* 6:118 (2015), [Sussex record](https://sussex.figshare.com/articles/journal_contribution/Explaining_mirror-touch_synesthesia_/23419469) |
| 6 | **Pain specifically.** When shown images and clips of injuries, **31 of 108** ordinary students (about 29%) felt pain, always in the **same body location** as the observed injury. | Osborn & Derbyshire, *Pain* 2010 (doi 10.1016/j.pain.2009.11.007), [IASP](https://www.iasp-pain.org/publications/relief-news/article/stuart-derbyshire-i-feel-your-pain/) |
| 7 | **16.2% of amputees surveyed** said that seeing or imagining someone else in pain triggers their phantom pain (n = 74 per a search summary; not confirmed in full text). The authors warn of false positives. | Fitzgibbon et al., *Neuropsychologia* 2010, "High incidence of 'synaesthesia for pain' in amputees"; the 16.2% figure is restated in the same group's [2011 follow-up, PMC3304487](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3304487) |

### Uncertain or disputed

- **The 1.6%** comes from one British student sample with a particular test, and other methods give other numbers. Say "**about 1 in 60, in one UK study**".
- **The empathy link is real but narrow**, limited to emotional empathy, and contested.

### Popular claims that are wrong

- "1 in 60 people feel others' pain." Mirror-touch is mostly **touch**, often mild.
- "Empaths absorb others' feelings." That's pop psychology, not mirror-touch.
- "Mirror neurons explain it." That is one of two competing theories.
- "Synaesthetes are more empathic in every way."

### Payoff number

**1.6%, about 1 in 60** ([Banissy 2009](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00776/pdf)). "In a big audience, some of you felt that." For a bigger number, about **29%** of students felt a flicker of pain watching injuries ([Osborn & Derbyshire 2010](https://www.iasp-pain.org/publications/relief-news/article/stuart-derbyshire-i-feel-your-pain/)); flag that it comes from a single study.
