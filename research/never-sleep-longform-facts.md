# Fact-check: "What if you never had to sleep?" (8 Nov 2026)

For the 6-minute second-person life story (birth to old age) and its "what's real" section. Every number below was checked against a paper (PubMed or PMC abstract or full text), a government or university page, or a major outlet quoting the paper. Where sources disagree, the disagreement is stated.

### Biggest traps

1. **"Rats/people die from lack of sleep after X days."** The rat deaths have no agreed cause. No healthy human has been documented to die from sleeplessness alone. FFI patients die from prion brain damage.
2. **"Sleep washes toxins from your brain."** This was settled pop-science in 2013–2023. Since Miao et al. (2024) it is openly disputed, so a pinned correction is likely if it is stated flatly.
3. **"Gardner was fine."** That rests on Dement's account. Ross, the Navy physician, documented slurred speech, memory lapses and a delusion. Dement later accepted that Gardner probably had microsleeps.
4. **"Natural short sleepers need 4 hours" / "DEC2 = 4 hours."** The DEC2 carriers averaged 6.25 h. The 4–6 h range is the lab's definition of the trait. A 2022 study of 191,929 people found the known variants did not shorten sleep in carriers from the general population.
5. **Old NSF newborn range "12–18 hours."** NSF changed it to 14–17 hours in 2015.

## 1. A third of life asleep, about 26 years by 80

### Verified

| Fact | Source |
|---|---|
| "Human beings spend one-third of their life asleep" is the standard framing in the research literature. | Eide et al., *Brain* 144:863 (2021), opening line of the abstract. [PubMed 33829232](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33829232/) |
| NSF ranges (hours/day): newborns 0–3 mo **14–17**; infants 4–11 mo **12–15**; toddlers 1–2 y **11–14**; preschool 3–5 y **10–13**; school age 6–13 y **9–11**; teens 14–17 y **8–10**; adults 18–64 **7–9**; 65+ **7–8**. | Hirshkowitz et al., *Sleep Health* 1:40 (2015). [PubMed 29073412](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29073412/) |
| Measured sleep changes with age. In adults, total sleep time, sleep efficiency and deep (slow-wave) sleep all decline with age (meta-analysis of 65 studies, 3,577 people aged 5–102). | Ohayon et al., *Sleep* 27:1255 (2004). [PubMed 15586779](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15586779/) |
| Among 1.1 million people, only 6.5% of adults reported sleeping under 6 h. One in four people slept less than their age-specific recommendation. | Kocevska et al., *Nat Hum Behav* 5:113 (2021). [PubMed 33199855](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33199855/) |

### Our arithmetic (NSF ranges, ages 0–80)

| Scenario | Years asleep by 80 | Share of life |
|---|---|---|
| Everyone at the **bottom** of their age range | 25.1 | 31% |
| Children at the **middle** of their range; adults at **7 h** (typical) | **26.0** | **32.5%** |
| Everyone at the **middle** of their range | 28.2 | 35% |
| Everyone at the **top** of their range | 31.4 | 39% |

- **First year of life:** at NSF midpoints a baby sleeps about **213 of 365 days' worth (58%)**. Even at the bottom of the range it is 190 days (52%). So "more than half of their first year" is safe.
- **Share of the day by age:** a newborn sleeps 58–71% of the day; an adult at 7–9 h sleeps 29–38%.
- **Verdict:** "about 26 years" is a fair round number. "26–27 years" is also fine. Avoid "27" as an exact figure, since it implies adults average about 8 h.

### Caution

- The US Bureau of Labor Statistics' American Time Use Survey reports **9.04 h/day "sleeping"** (2024, ages 15+). That category includes naps and time lying awake trying to sleep, so it is not real sleep time. Don't use it to argue for "37% of life asleep." [BLS ATUS](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/atus.nr0.htm)
- Viral add-ons like "...and 7 years trying to fall asleep" come from mattress-company surveys. Avoid them.

## 2. Infant sleep need ("you never napped as a baby")

| Fact | Source |
|---|---|
| NSF: newborns (0–3 months) **14–17 h**; infants (4–11 months) **12–15 h**. The 2015 update narrowed the old newborn range of 12–18 h. | Hirshkowitz et al. 2015 [PubMed 29073412](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29073412/) |
| AASM (endorsed by the American Academy of Pediatrics): infants **4–12 months: 12–16 h per 24 h, including naps**; 1–2 y 11–14 h; 3–5 y 10–13 h; 6–12 y 9–12 h; 13–18 y 8–10 h. | Paruthi et al., *J Clin Sleep Med* 12:785 (2016). [PMC4877308](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4877308/); [PDF](https://aasm.org/resources/pdf/pediatricsleepdurationconsensus.pdf) |
| AASM footnote: "Recommendations for infants younger than 4 months are not included due to the wide range of normal variation in duration and patterns of sleep." | Same PDF |
| Observed sleep (34 studies): infants average **12.8 h** (normal range 9.7–15.9); toddlers/preschool 11.9 h; children 9.2 h. | Galland et al., *Sleep Med Rev* 16:213 (2012). [PubMed 21784676](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21784676/) |

**Safe line:** "A newborn normally sleeps 14 to 17 hours a day." If you cite the AASM, say "babies from four months to a year need 12 to 16 hours, naps included." Don't attribute a newborn figure to the AASM.

## 3. Natural short sleepers (DEC2 and others)

### DEC2 / BHLHE41 (He et al., *Science* 2009)

- **Finding:** a mutation, **hDEC2-P385R** (proline to arginine at position 385), in a transcriptional repressor. It was found in two members of one family. UCSF press coverage describes them as a mother and her adult daughter. The family was identified while screening families with advanced sleep phase.
- **Hours:** "habitual self-reported total sleep time per 24-hour day was much shorter in mutation carriers (**average 6.25 hours**) compared with the noncarriers (**average 8.06 hours**) in this family." The change was not found in over 250 control samples. Note these are self-reported hours.
- **Animals:** transgenic mice carrying the mutation slept less and stayed active about 1.2 h longer. Transgenic flies also slept less.
- **Later:** a second DEC2 variant (Y362H) appeared in one twin of a pair (Pellegrino et al., *Sleep* 2014). That twin slept less, needed less recovery sleep and had fewer lapses after sleep deprivation than his co-twin. [PubMed 25083013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25083013/)
- Source: [PMC2884988](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2884988/)

### Other familial natural short sleep (FNSS) genes

| Gene | Paper | Key numbers |
|---|---|---|
| **ADRB1** (A187V) | Shi et al., *Neuron* 103:1044 (2019). [PMC6763376](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6763376/) | One family, three generations. Carriers "on average sleep 2 hours/day less than non-mutation carriers." Variant frequency **4.028 per 100,000** (ExAC). One carrier slept 7.5 h, so penetrance is incomplete. Mice slept about 55 min less. |
| **NPSR1** (Y206H) | Xing et al., *Science Translational Medicine* 11:eaax2014 (2019). [PMC7587149](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7587149/) | Two carriers slept **5.5 h and 4.3 h**. Variant frequency **4.06 × 10⁻⁶** (about 4 per million, gnomAD). Mutant mice were protected from memory deficits after sleep deprivation. |
| **GRM1** (two variants) | Shi et al., ***Current Biology* 31:13 (2021)**, online Oct 2020. **Not 2022.** [PubMed 33065013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33065013/) | Two unrelated families. Variant frequencies 2.52 × 10⁻⁵ and 1.59 × 10⁻⁴. |
| **SIK3** (N783Y) | Chen et al., *PNAS* 122:e2500356122 (2025). [PubMed 40324078](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40324078/) | Newest addition. Mutant mice slept less, with higher delta power. |

### How rare is natural short sleep?

- **No reliable population prevalence exists.** Each variant is known from one or a few families. The variants themselves are rare in databases, roughly 1 in 25,000 for ADRB1 and about 4 in a million for NPSR1.
- The Fu/Ptáček lab defines FNSS as a "life-long tendency to sleep only **4–6 hours**/night and to feel well rested" (Shi 2019; Xing 2019).
- **Important caveat.** Weedon et al. (*PLoS Genetics* 2022) studied 191,929 people (UK Biobank, FINRISK, Health 2000, MESA). They found carriers for several of the reported variants, including DEC2, ADRB1 and GRM1, and **found no association with short sleep**. No NPSR1 carriers turned up. The authors conclude these variants "are often not highly penetrant when ascertained incidentally from the general population." [PubMed 36137075](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36137075/)
- **Verdict on "about 6 hours":** right for DEC2 (6.25 h). For the whole group, say "about 4 to 6 hours." Avoid percentages like "1% of people."

## 4. Randy Gardner (1963–64)

|---|---|---|

The last recognised record was **Robert McDonald, 453 h 40 min (18 days 21 h 40 min), 1986**, set in a rocking-chair marathon. **NPR says 1996**; trust Guinness's own 1997. | [Guinness World Records, Jan 2023](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/news/2023/1/whats-the-limit-to-how-long-a-human-can-stay-awake-733188) |

**Avoid:** "16 years old" and "1965." ScienceAlert has both wrong; 1965 is the publication year of Ross's paper. Also avoid "no effects at all."

## 5. Rechtschaffen's rat studies

| Fact | Source |
|---|---|
| **1983:** the first controlled demonstration. Experimental and yoked control rats got the same mild stimuli, timed to cut sleep only in the experimental rats. "Experimental rats suffered severe pathology and death; control rats did not." A later review gives the deprivation needed to reach a moribund state as **16 days** in the 1983 report, **averaging 21 days** in later studies. | Rechtschaffen, Gilliland, Bergmann & Winter, *Science* 221:182 (1983) [PubMed 6857280](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6857280/); Everson, *Behav Brain Res* 69:43 (1995) |
| **1989, total sleep deprivation:** "All TSD rats died or were sacrificed when death seemed imminent within **11–32 days**. **No anatomical cause of death was identified.**" They lost weight despite eating more. Energy expenditure reached "more than twice baseline." | Everson, Bergmann & Rechtschaffen, *Sleep* 12:13 (1989), n=10. [PubMed 2928622](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2928622/) |
| **REM-only deprivation:** death or near death within **16–54 days**; again no anatomical cause. | Kushida et al., *Sleep* 12:22 (1989), n=12. [PubMed 2928623](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2928623/) |
| The overall syndrome: death, debilitated look, skin lesions on paws and tail, more eating, weight loss, higher energy use, **falling body temperature late on**, higher noradrenaline, lower thyroxine. The authors suggest "sleep may be necessary for effective thermoregulation." | Rechtschaffen et al., *Sleep* 12:68 (1989) [PubMed 2648533](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2648533/); reprinted *Sleep* 2002 |
| **Comparison:** the timeline to a moribund state is similar to food deprivation (17–19 days). | Everson 1995 |
| **Later clue: infection.** Blood cultures showed "invasion by opportunistic microbes" with no fever. Bacteria appeared first in the gut's lymph nodes, "presumably translocated from the intestine." The end point was "fatal bloodstream infection without an infectious focus." | Everson, *Am J Physiol* 1993 [PubMed 8238617](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8238617/); Everson & Toth, *Am J Physiol* 2000 [PubMed 10749778](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10749778/) |
| **Vaccaro et al., *Cell* 2020 (Harvard):** in flies and mice, sleep loss causes reactive oxygen species (ROS) to build up "specifically in the gut." In **flies**, neutralising ROS with oral antioxidants or gut-targeted enzymes "allows flies to have a normal lifespan with little to no sleep." **Mice** were kept awake for up to 5 days and showed gut ROS, but mouse survival was not tested. The abstract opens by calling the cause of lethality "unknown." A patent is pending. | [PubMed 32502393](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32502393/); [Harvard Brain Science Initiative](https://brain.harvard.edu/hbi_news/why-severe-sleep-deprivation-can-be-lethal/) |

**Narrative gift:** the 2000 rat data (gut bacteria leaking out) and the 2020 fly data (the gut as the site of damage) point the same way. Safe line: "Decades later, scientists found an unexpected clue in the gut." Don't claim the gut explains human sleep need.

## 6. Fatal familial insomnia (FFI)

| Fact | Source |
|---|---|
| A hereditary **prion disease** caused by a mutation at codon 178 of the prion protein gene (**PRNP D178N**). It requires **methionine at codon 129** on the mutant allele; D178N with valine there causes a form of CJD instead. It is autosomal dominant. A sporadic form (sporadic fatal insomnia) also exists. | Montagna, Gambetti, Cortelli & Lugaresi, *Lancet Neurol* 2:167 (2003) [PubMed 12849238](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12849238/) |
| First described in 1986 in an Italian family. | Lugaresi et al., *NEJM* 315:997 (1986) [PubMed 3762620](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3762620/) |
| Features: loss of sleep spindles and slow-wave sleep, acted-out dreams, autonomic overactivity (high blood pressure was seen in 33.6% of cases in the 2022 series), and motor problems. There is severe neuron loss in the **anterior medial thalamus**. | Montagna et al. 2003 |
| **Largest case series (131 genetically confirmed cases worldwide):** onset **47.5 ± 12.5 years (range 17–76)**; disease duration **13.2 ± 9.0 months (range 2–48)**. Insomnia was present in **87%**, so not every patient leads with insomnia. Rapidly progressive dementia in 83%. Met/Met at codon 129 in 84.7%; Met/Val cases lasted longer. | Zhang et al., *J Neurol Neurosurg Psychiatry* 93:291 (2022) [PMC8862016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8862016/) |
| **Families:** "Up until 2016, more than hundred FFI cases from **50 families** … have been reported," mostly from Italy, Spain and Germany. Thirteen Chinese families were documented from 2006 to 2017. Wikipedia's "70 families (2022)" is unverified. | Chinese expert consensus, *Chin Med J* 131:1613 (2018) [PMC6032681](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6032681/) |

**Safe wording:** "a few dozen families worldwide" and "usually fatal within one to two years of the first symptoms." Many popular sources say "18 months." The 131-case review's mean is about 13 months.

**Avoid:** "FFI patients die because they can't sleep." The disease is prion neurodegeneration, and sleep loss is one symptom. Also avoid "they never sleep at all"; sleep becomes fragmented and abnormal rather than absent.

## 7. Glymphatic clearance (flag as disputed)

| Fact | Source |
|---|---|
| **Xie et al. 2013, mice:** "natural sleep or anesthesia are associated with a **60% increase in the interstitial space**." Tracer influx was about 95% lower in awake mice than in sleeping ones. "Aβ was cleared **twofold faster** in the sleeping mice as compared with the awake mice." | Xie et al., *Science* 342:373 (2013) [PMC3880190](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3880190/) |
| **Challenge (Imperial College London):** "brain clearance is **markedly reduced, not increased**, during sleep and anesthesia." Dye clearance was about **30% lower in sleeping mice** and about **50% lower under anaesthesia** than in awake mice. | Miao et al., *Nat Neurosci* 27:1046 (2024) [PMC11156584](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11156584/); [UK DRI press release](https://ukdri.ac.uk/news-and-events/scientists-find-sleep-may-not-clear-brain-toxins-as-previously-thought) |
| An earlier challenge found solute movement through brain tissue is diffusive and does not depend on AQP4. "Our results do not support the proposed glymphatic mechanism." | Smith et al., *eLife* 2017 [PubMed 28826498](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28826498/) |
| **Counter-evidence (pro):** noradrenaline-driven blood-vessel pulsing pumps CSF into the brain during NREM sleep. The sleep drug zolpidem suppressed it. | Hauglund et al., *Cell* 188:606 (2025) [PubMed 39788123](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39788123/) |
| **Human data (small):** one night without sleep impaired clearance of an MRI tracer from most brain regions, and the next night's sleep did not make up for it. Only 7 people were sleep-deprived, against 17 controls. | Eide et al., *Brain* 2021 [PubMed 33829232](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33829232/) |

**Safe wording:** "Some scientists think sleep helps flush waste from the brain, but it's one of the hottest arguments in neuroscience right now." Don't say "your brain cleans itself only when you sleep."

## 8. Half-brain sleep and sleeping in flight

| Fact | Source |
|---|---|
| Unihemispheric slow-wave sleep has been seen "in **all members of the order Cetacea examined to date**." Cetaceans have little or no REM sleep and keep one eye open, on the side of the awake hemisphere. It was first recorded in dolphins in 1977. | Lyamin et al., *Neurosci Biobehav Rev* 32:1451 (2008) [PubMed 18602158](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18602158/); Mukhametov et al., *Brain Res* 1977 [PubMed 902119](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/902119/) |
| Two dolphins echolocated and reported targets **continuously for 5 days**, and one for **15 days**, without decline. | Branstetter et al., *PLoS One* 2012 [PubMed 23082170](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23082170/) |
| **Northern fur seals:** normal two-sided sleep on land, but asymmetric (one-hemisphere) sleep in seawater. They go days to weeks with almost no REM sleep in the water, with little or no REM rebound afterwards. | Lyamin et al., *Curr Biol* 28:2000 (2018) [PubMed 29887309](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29887309/) |
| **Great frigatebirds** (EEG on birds flying up to 10 days): they can sleep with one hemisphere **or both at once** in flight. Sleep in flight is **0.69 h/day (≈41 min)**, 7.4% of their sleep on land. Sleep took 2.89% of flight time versus **53.28% of time on land (≈12.8 h/day)**. In flight, sleep episodes averaged about **11–12 seconds** and the longest was 348 s. Sleep happened almost only at night, mostly while circling in rising air. | Rattenborg et al., *Nat Commun* 7:12468 (2016) [PMC4976198](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4976198/) |
| **Bonus:** northern elephant seals at sea took sub-20-minute naps while diving (to 377 m) and averaged **about 2 hours of sleep per day for 7 months**. That rivals the African elephant (about 2 h/day) for the least sleep of any mammal. | Kendall-Bar et al., *Science* 380:260 (2023) [PubMed 37079694](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37079694/) |

**Verdict on "about 42 minutes vs more than 12 hours":** correct. 0.69 h is 41.4 min, and 53.28% of 24 h is 12.8 h. "About 40 minutes" is the safest phrasing.

## 9. Microsleeps and drowsy driving

| Fact | Source |
|---|---|
| **Microsleeps** are "short fragments of sleep (**1–15 s**) that can cause dangerous situations with potentially fatal outcomes." There is still no standard definition. | Skorucak et al., *Front Neurosci* 14:8 (2020) [PubMed 32038155](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32038155/) |
| **Official US count:** "NHTSA reported that **633 people were killed in drowsy driving crashes in 2023**." That is 1.5% of fatal crashes, and police reports "substantially underestimate drowsy driving." | Governors Highway Safety Association, *Wake Up Call: 2026 Update* [PDF](https://www.ghsa.org/sites/default/files/2026-02/Wake_Up_Call_2026_Update.pdf) |
| **Older official figure (2017):** 91,000 police-reported crashes, about 50,000 injuries and about 800 deaths. | NHTSA, as cited by the AAA Foundation. The NHTSA page returned 403 to us. |
| **Model estimate:** "an estimated **17.6% of all fatal crashes** in years 2017–2021 involved a drowsy driver." That is an estimated **29,834 deaths** over 5 years, about 6,000 a year. GHSA applies the same share to 2023 and gets **6,326 deaths**, "ten times more than the raw FARS data." | Tefft, AAA Foundation for Traffic Safety (March 2024) [link](https://aaafoundation.org/drowsy-driving-in-fatal-crashes-united-states-2017-2021/); GHSA 2026 |
| **Dash-cam study:** drowsiness was found in **8.8–9.5% of all crashes** and 10.6–10.8% of crashes with airbag deployment, injury or major damage. Drivers counted as drowsy if their eyes were closed in at least 12% of video frames. The sample was 701 crashes from 3,593 drivers monitored 2010–13. | Owens et al., AAA Foundation (Feb 2018) [link](https://aaafoundation.org/prevalence-drowsy-driving-crashes-estimates-large-scale-naturalistic-driving-study/) |

**Safe wording:** "Police reports blame drowsy driving for about 600 US road deaths a year. Researchers who correct for under-reporting estimate it's closer to 6,000." Always label the 6,000 as an estimate.

### Do not use (unverified, myth, or misleading)

- "Thai Ngoc hasn't slept since 1962." This is the subject of Drew Binsky's video (26M views). It has never been verified in a sleep lab.
- "Humans die after X days without sleep." There is no verified human case.
- "Peter Tripp / Tony Wright / the streamer record" as facts. These are poorly documented, and Guinness doesn't monitor the record. Wright's claimed record has never been verified.
- "Sleep cleans your brain" stated flatly (see §7).
- "Dolphins never sleep." They do, one hemisphere at a time.
- "The Russian Sleep Experiment." It is creepypasta fiction.
